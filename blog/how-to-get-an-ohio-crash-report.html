How to Get an Ohio Crash Report (OH-1): Where to Look and How Long It Takes

By Tyler Tevis August 2026 8 min read

Every reportable traffic crash in Ohio produces the same piece of paper: form OH-1, the Ohio Traffic Crash Report. Whether you are a driver who needs a copy for an insurance claim, a firm evaluating a case, or a business that works with crash data at volume, the way you get it is the same request process.

Here is how that process actually works, including the parts that are not obvious until you have done it a few times.

What the OH-1 Is

The OH-1 is a standard statewide form. Every law enforcement agency in Ohio files the same one — the Highway Patrol, county sheriffs, and city police departments all use it. A report from Lakewood PD has the same fields in the same places as a report from a Highway Patrol post.

That standardization matters more than it sounds. It is the only reason crash data can be aggregated across the state at all. If every department used its own form, there would be no such thing as an Ohio crash dataset.

Not every collision produces one. A report is generally filed when there is an injury, significant property damage, or a vehicle that has to be towed. Two drivers who exchange information in a parking lot and never call anyone do not generate an OH-1.

Who Has Your Report

There are two places to look, and which one is faster depends on who worked the crash.

  • The Ohio Department of Public Safety operates a statewide crash report retrieval system that carries reports submitted electronically by agencies across Ohio. If the Highway Patrol worked your crash, this is almost always the fastest route.
  • The investigating agency itself. Every department keeps its own copy. Some publish through their own online portal, some through a third-party records vendor, and some still want you to walk into the records division.

If a city police department worked the crash, check both. Local reports do reach the state system, but not always immediately, and a department's own portal sometimes has it first.

The one thing you need before you start looking is the report number, or failing that, the date, the county, and the name of one person involved. The officer at the scene usually hands over a card with the report number on it.

How Long Before It Is Available

Not immediately, and this is where most of the frustration comes from. The officer writes the report, a supervisor reviews it, and only then is it submitted. Nothing appears in any portal before that review is done.

A typical window is a few days to about two weeks. Fatal and serious-injury crashes take longer, because they get more scrutiny, and they are sometimes withheld entirely while a criminal investigation is open.

If it has been more than two weeks, stop refreshing the portal and call the agency's records division. A report that is stuck is usually stuck for a reason someone there can tell you in thirty seconds.

What It Costs

Cost varies by agency. Retrieving a report through the state's online system is typically free. Local agencies differ — some post reports online at no charge, some charge a per-copy fee at the counter.

Request procedures differ too. If you are unsure what a particular department charges or what it wants from you, its records division will tell you in a phone call. Following that agency's own process is faster than assuming a general rule applies.

A practical note. Agencies are usually helpful when you make their job easy. Give them the report number, the date, and one name. Vague requests get slow answers, not because anyone is obstructing you, but because someone has to go find it.

What Is Removed Before Release

Not every field on the officer's copy reaches the copy you receive. Agencies withhold or mask some information before release, and what comes out varies with the circumstances of the crash. Reports tied to an open investigation are the most common case.

What generally does survive on a released report:

  • Crash date, time, and location
  • Investigating agency and report number
  • The units involved and their damage
  • The people involved, with name and address as given at the scene
  • An injury severity code for each person
  • The officer's narrative and diagram

That last set is why crash reports are useful to anyone beyond the people in the crash. It is also why they are handled carefully. What you may do with the data is a separate question from what you can obtain, and the answer depends on your industry and your intended use. That one is worth settling with your own counsel before you build anything on top of it.

If the Report Is Wrong

Errors are common. Misspelled names, transposed addresses, an injury coded conservatively at the scene by an officer who is not a physician.

You cannot edit a filed report. What you can do is contact the investigating agency's records division and ask about a supplemental or amended report. The original stays in the file; the correction is added to it.

Insurance carriers treat the OH-1 as a starting point rather than a verdict, so a wrong report is worth correcting but is not the last word on anything.

Working With Crash Reports at Volume

One report is a form you read in two minutes. Ten thousand reports is a dataset, and nothing about the request process is designed to hand you one.

Agencies publish reports one at a time, as PDFs, with the fields you would actually want locked inside a document rather than sitting in a table. Getting from "these records are public" to "these records are a spreadsheet" is a real amount of work, and it is the same work every single time.
