How Much Does a Small Business Website Cost? A Cleveland Owner’s Guide

By Tyler Tevis July 2026 9 min read

“How much does a small business website cost?” is the first question almost every owner asks us — and it’s the hardest one to answer in a sentence, because the honest range runs from a few hundred dollars to well into five figures. That spread is why so many Cleveland business owners either overpay for something they didn’t need or underpay for something that quietly costs them customers for years.

So let’s take the mystery out of it. Below is what a small business website actually costs in 2026, what drives the number up or down, the ongoing costs nobody mentions in the sales pitch, and how to tell which tier you genuinely belong in. No hedging, no “it depends” without an explanation.

The Four Real Price Tiers

Nearly every quote you’ll get falls into one of four buckets. Knowing which one you’re shopping in is most of the battle.

  • DIY builders ($0–$400/year): Wix, Squarespace, GoDaddy. You do the work. Fine for a hobby or a placeholder, and genuinely the right answer for some very small operations.
  • Freelance / low-end template work ($500–$1,500): Someone drops your logo and copy into a purchased theme. Fast and cheap, but usually thin on strategy, speed, and follow-up support.
  • Professional small business site ($1,250–$5,000): A properly built brochure or lead-generation site — custom layout, real copywriting, mobile-first, fast, search-optimized, and built so someone can actually maintain it. Our own brochure sites start at $1,250. This is where most Cleveland small businesses belong.
  • E-commerce, booking, or custom-application sites ($5,000–$25,000+): Online stores, client portals, scheduling systems, inventory or payment integrations. The price reflects real software work, not page design.

The uncomfortable truth about the cheap tier: A $600 template site and a $2,500 professional site often look similar in a screenshot. The difference shows up in load speed, mobile behavior, whether Google can find you, and whether anyone answers the phone in eight months when something breaks. You’re buying the parts you can’t see in the screenshot.

What Actually Drives the Cost Up

When a quote comes back higher than you expected, it’s almost always one of these five things — and each one is worth understanding before you cut it to save money.

  • Page count and unique layouts. Five pages that share a template is a very different job from fifteen pages that each need their own design.
  • Content. If you supply finished text and photos, you save real money. If someone has to interview you, write your service pages, and source imagery, that’s hours — often the single biggest line item.
  • Functionality. A contact form is trivial. Online payments, appointment booking, customer logins, or syncing with your existing software are engineering work, and they’re priced that way.
  • Integrations. Connecting your site to a CRM, an email list, a scheduling tool, or a point-of-sale system adds scope every time.
  • SEO depth. Basic on-page structure should be included in any competent build. Deeper local SEO work — keyword research for Lakewood, Parma, Westlake, or Shaker Heights, location pages, schema markup, Google Business Profile alignment — is additional but frequently the highest-return money you’ll spend.

The Ongoing Costs Nobody Warns You About

The build price is the headline; the running costs are what surprise people in year two. Budget for a domain name ($15–$25/year), hosting ($10–$40/month for a typical small business site, sometimes free on modern static hosting), an SSL certificate (usually included now — if someone charges you separately for it, ask why), and any premium plugins or licenses your site depends on.

Then there’s maintenance, which is the one most people skip and later regret. Software updates, security patches, backups, broken-link checks, form testing, and uptime monitoring aren’t optional on a site that represents your business. Our care plans start at $79/month for a straightforward brochure site and $139/month for a site that’s actively generating leads or sales — and if we built the site, those drop to $59 and $119 respectively, for as long as you stay with us.

What a Cleveland Small Business Should Actually Budget

Here’s the practical answer we give owners across Northeast Ohio. A local service business — an HVAC contractor in Parma, a dental practice in Shaker Heights, a law office in Westlake — that needs a credible, findable, lead-generating site should plan on roughly $1,500–$4,000 up front and $80–$150/month ongoing. That gets you a fast, mobile-first, search-ready site with your services, service area, proof, and a working way to contact you, plus someone keeping it alive.

If you’re selling products online or need booking and customer accounts, start your planning at $6,000 and expect the number to move with complexity. And if you’re a brand-new solo operation testing whether the business works at all, a DIY builder plus a well-optimized Google Business Profile is a perfectly respectable place to start — we’ll tell you that rather than sell you a site you’re not ready for.

Why the Cheapest Quote Usually Isn’t the Cheapest Website

We rebuild a lot of sites that were cheap the first time. The pattern is remarkably consistent: a bargain build comes in slow and bloated, it isn’t structured so Google can understand what the business does or where it operates, the developer becomes unreachable, and nobody has the login credentials. Two years later the owner pays again — this time for a rebuild plus the recovery work of untangling a site nobody documented.

The same logic applies to inheriting an existing site. Taking over an unfamiliar codebase honestly takes time to understand before anyone can safely maintain it, which is why we scope a small one-time discovery step for third-party sites and waive it entirely on sites we built. It’s not a fee for the sake of a fee — it’s the difference between someone who knows your site and someone guessing.

Questions to Ask Before You Sign Anything

Whoever you hire — us or someone else — get clear answers to these first:

  1. Who owns the domain, the hosting account, and the site files? The answer must be “you.” Get the credentials in writing.
  2. Is the price fixed, or hourly? Fixed-price scope protects you from the meter running.
  3. What’s included after launch, and what costs extra? Get the maintenance terms before the build starts, not after.
  4. Can I update my own content? If every text change requires a paid ticket, that’s a long-term cost.
  5. What are the mobile and page-speed targets? More than half your visitors are on a phone, and speed affects both rankings and whether people stay.
